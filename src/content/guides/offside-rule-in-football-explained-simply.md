Few rules cause more arguments than offside, and yet the idea behind it is simple: attackers should not be able to wait next to the goal for a long pass. The Laws of the Game, written and maintained by the International Football Association Board (IFAB), define it in Law 11. This guide walks through that law step by step, so a goal ruled out by a few centimetres makes sense.

## Step one: the offside position

Being in an offside position is not a foul. A player is in an offside position when both of these are true at the moment a teammate plays or touches the ball:

1. Any part of their head, body or feet is in the opponents' half of the pitch, not counting the halfway line.
2. Any part of their head, body or feet is nearer to the opponents' goal line than both the ball and the second-last opponent.

The goalkeeper usually counts as one of the two opponents, which is why the rule is often summed up as "level with or behind the last defender". Strictly, it is the second-last opponent that matters, and either of them can be an outfield player.

## Which body parts count

The law lists the parts of the body that can be offside: head, body and feet. Hands and arms, including the goalkeeper's, are not considered: for this purpose the arm ends at the bottom of the armpit. That is why you may see a replay with a striker's outstretched arm beyond the line and no offside given.

## Step two: the offside offence

Standing in an offside position only matters if the player then becomes involved in active play. The law gives three ways of doing that:

- **Interfering with play** by playing or touching a ball passed or touched by a teammate.
- **Interfering with an opponent**, for example by blocking their line of sight, challenging them for the ball or clearly attempting to play a ball that is close to them when this affects the opponent.
- **Gaining an advantage** by playing a ball that rebounds or is deflected off the goalpost, the crossbar, a match official or an opponent after being in the offside position.

If none of those happens, play continues. A forward can stand beyond the line, watch a teammate shoot and score, and the goal stands.

## When offside cannot be given

A player is not committing an offence if they receive the ball directly from:

- a goal kick
- a throw-in
- a corner kick

There is also no offside in a player's own half, and being level with the second-last defender is onside. A ball played backwards by the attacking side can still make a teammate offside, so the direction of the pass does not matter; only the positions at the moment of the pass do.

## What the referee awards

For an offside offence the referee gives an indirect free kick to the defending team. It is taken from where the offending player became involved in play, which may be somewhere other than where they were standing when the ball was played. There is no direct shot at goal from this kick.

## How technology checks it

Offside decisions are among those reviewed by the video assistant referee (VAR) in matches where it is used. Lines are drawn on a still frame at the moment of the pass, and for years the question of exactly which frame to use was debated. In recent years competitions have added semi-automated offside technology, first used at a men's World Cup in Qatar in 2022, which uses cameras and a sensor in the ball to speed up the check and build a 3D picture of the players. Wherever the system is used, the call is still bound by the same wording of the law.

## Common misunderstandings

**"He was ahead of the ball, so he is offside."** An attacker can be in front of the ball and still be onside if two defenders are nearer their own goal line.

**"Offside is judged when he receives the ball."** The moment that counts is when the teammate plays or touches the ball, not when it arrives.

**"If the defender plays the ball, offside is reset."** A deliberate play by an opponent, other than a deliberate save, can reset the situation. A deflection or rebound does not.

**"The offside line is a straight line across the pitch."** It runs parallel to the goal line through the second-last opponent, though broadcast graphics can look skewed because of camera perspective.

## Why this matters for predictions

Offside is mostly a matter for fans and officials, but it explains some patterns that affect scoring.
