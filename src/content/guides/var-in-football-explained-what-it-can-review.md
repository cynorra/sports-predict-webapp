Few modern changes to football have caused as much argument as the video assistant referee. Fans see a goal disallowed after a long wait, a penalty awarded from a replay, or a close call that nobody agrees on. Much of the frustration comes from not knowing what VAR is allowed to do. The rules are narrower than many assume, and understanding them explains most of what happens on screen.

## What VAR is

VAR is a team of officials watching the match on video, in a room away from the pitch. They are not a second referee. Their job is to check certain decisions and tell the on-field referee when there may have been a clear mistake. The principle behind the system, set out by the International Football Association Board (IFAB), is minimum interference and maximum benefit: review only when the outcome could change the result.

## The four reviewable situations

VAR may become involved only in these match-changing situations:

1. **Goals.** Including offences by the attacking team during the build-up, such as offside, a handball or a foul.
2. **Penalty decisions.** Awarded or not, including offences inside the area and the position of the offence.
3. **Direct red cards.** A sending-off for serious foul play, violent conduct or denying a goal-scoring opportunity, but not a second yellow card.
4. **Mistaken identity.** When the referee cautions or sends off the wrong player.

## How a check works

Every incident in those categories is checked silently in the background. In most cases nothing happens, and play continues. If the video assistant sees a possible clear and obvious error, there are two routes:

- **A factual decision**, such as whether the ball crossed the line or whether an attacker was in an offside position, can be corrected by the video assistant telling the referee, who then changes the decision.
- **A subjective decision**, such as the severity of a challenge, usually leads to an on-field review: the referee goes to a pitchside monitor and watches the replay before deciding.

Only the referee can signal a review with the "TV" gesture and announce the final decision.

## The clear and obvious standard

For subjective decisions, the video assistant will only recommend a review when the original call is clearly wrong. If a challenge could be judged either way and the referee had a reasonable view, the on-field decision stands. That is why two similar challenges can end with different outcomes, and why replays do not always lead to a change.

## What VAR does not cover

Many incidents sit outside the four categories:

- Yellow cards, except for mistaken identity.
- Awards of corner kicks and throw-ins, which the referee judges alone.
- Fouls in open play that do not lead to a goal, a penalty or a direct red card.
- Added time, which remains the referee's call.

Competitions may adapt the details of how the system is run, so the practical routine differs a little between tournaments.

## Offside and technology

Offside checks use camera angles and a line drawn on a frame at the moment the ball is played. Newer semi-automated systems, first used at a men's World Cup in 2022, track player positions to speed the check. The wording of the law is unchanged, as described in the [offside guide](/guides/offside-rule-in-football-explained-simply).

## Why decisions still cause debate

Even with replays, some decisions remain a matter of opinion. A challenge can look reckless from one angle and fair from another, and the law leaves room for judgement. The clear and obvious standard also means that a decision the referee could reasonably have made stays, even if many viewers disagree. Finally, the check works on single video frames, so a player can be ruled offside by a margin smaller than the gap between two frames. These are features of the process rather than signs that it is broken, but they explain why VAR remains controversial.

## What VAR changed for scores

Video review aims to remove clear errors, and it also changes how matches flow: goals take longer to confirm and added time can be longer. Whether it changes the number of goals scored is hard to isolate, and the [Poisson model](/guides/poisson-distribution-football-goals-explained) used on this site makes no adjustment for it. When you pick scores on the [prediction page](/predict), treat a close offside or penalty call as a coin flip, not as something to build a pick on.

## Where to read the protocol

IFAB publishes the video assistant referee protocol as part of the Laws of the Game. It sets out the four categories, the procedures and the rule that the referee makes the final decision. The full text is on the [IFAB website](https://www.theifab.com).
