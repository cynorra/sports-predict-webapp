Before kickoff, broadcasters flash a graphic with numbers such as 4-2-3-1. It looks like code, but it is only a quick way to describe how a team lines up. Knowing how to read it helps you follow lineups, and it clarifies why managers talk about "changing shape" during a match. This guide covers football formations: the notation, the three classic shapes and what a shape can and cannot tell you.

## How to read the numbers

A formation lists outfield players in rows, starting from the defence. The goalkeeper is assumed, so the numbers always add up to ten.

- **4-4-2**: four defenders, four midfielders, two forwards.
- **4-3-3**: four defenders, three midfielders, three forwards.
- **3-5-2**: three defenders, five midfielders, two forwards.

Some formations have four numbers, such as 4-2-3-1. They split the midfield into a deeper pair and an attacking trio behind a lone striker. The more numbers, the finer the description.

## 4-4-2: two banks of four

The 4-4-2 is the shape many fans grew up with. Two compact lines of four protect the goal, and two strikers work together up front.

- **Strengths:** simple to understand, hard to play through in the middle, two strikers give a target and a runner.
- **Weaknesses:** the two central midfielders can be outnumbered by a trio, so teams using it often defend deep and counter.

## 4-3-3: width and a midfield trio

The 4-3-3 puts three midfielders behind a front three: a central forward and two wide attackers. Full-backs often push forward to give extra width.

- **Strengths:** a trio in midfield makes it easier to keep the ball, and wide forwards stretch the pitch.
- **Weaknesses:** if the wide forwards do not track back, the full-backs are exposed. The lone central forward can be isolated.

## 3-5-2: wing-backs and a back three

A back three leaves room for two wing-backs who cover the whole flank. With the ball they act like wingers; without it they drop into a back five.

- **Strengths:** an extra central defender, a crowded midfield and two strikers.
- **Weaknesses:** the wing-backs must cover huge distances, and the space behind them is a target for fast counter-attacks.

## Shapes change during a match

A formation is a starting layout, not a fixed position. A team may attack in a 3-2-5 shape and defend in a 4-4-2 or 5-3-2, depending on where the ball is. Managers also change shape after a goal or a red card. This is why two teams listed as 4-3-3 can play very differently, and why a formation tag on its own predicts little.

## What formations do and do not tell you

**They do not decide who wins.** A team of better players usually beats a team with a smarter-looking formation. Quality gaps measured by a rating such as [Elo](/guides/elo-ratings-in-football-explained) explain much more of the result than the numbers on a graphic.

**They affect how space appears.** A back three against two strikers has an extra player in defence. A midfield trio outnumbers a pair. Those small imbalances create chances, which are measured in [expected goals](/guides/what-is-xg-expected-goals-explained).

**They affect the offside line.** A high defensive line squeezes the space in front of the goal, but it relies on good timing to catch attackers offside.

## Other shapes you will hear about

- **4-2-3-1:** a lone striker, three attacking midfielders behind and a pair of holding midfielders. It is popular because it balances defence and attack in the middle.
- **4-1-4-1:** a single holding midfielder shields the defence and a line of four midfielders supports one striker.
- **5-3-2 or 5-4-1:** a back five, often a defensive choice against stronger opponents.
- **3-4-3:** three defenders, four midfielders and three attackers, an attacking setup that depends on wide players covering a lot of ground.

None of these is better in the abstract. Each answers a different question about how many players to commit forward and where to put them.

## Using formations in predictions

For a score prediction, the useful question is whether the lineup changes a team's expected goals. A few cases do:

1. **Missing key players.** A formation built around a specific striker or playmaker may lose its logic without them.
2. **A switch to a more cautious shape.** A team that adds a defender may score less and concede less, pushing the total goals down.
3. **A late tactical change.** Lineups are usually confirmed about an hour before kickoff, so check them before locking a pick on the [prediction page](/predict).

Treat these as modest adjustments. The [score prediction guide](/guides/how-to-predict-football-scores) places lineups after team quality and home advantage for that reason.

## Further reading

The Laws of the Game do not mention formations at all, since teams may line up as they wish within the rules.
