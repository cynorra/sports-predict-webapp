The World Cup top scorers list changed at the 2026 tournament for the first time in twelve years. Kylian Mbappé now leads the men's all-time list with 22 goals, Lionel Messi is second with 21, and Miroslav Klose, who held the record from 2014, is third with 16. This guide sets out the list, shows how the record has changed hands since 1958 and explains why goals per match is a fairer comparison than raw totals.

All figures come from FIFA's own records and from the Opta Analyst's ranking published after the tournament. They count goals at World Cup final tournaments, not qualifying matches.

## The all-time list

At the top of the table, FIFA and the Opta Analyst agree: Mbappé 22, Messi 21, Klose 16 and Ronaldo of Brazil 15. Gerd Müller of West Germany and Harry Kane of England share fifth place on 14, followed by Just Fontaine on 13 and Pelé on 12. Opta then lists Sándor Kocsis, Jürgen Klinsmann and Cristiano Ronaldo on 11 each, with six players on 10.

The two Ronaldos cause most of the confusion. The Brazilian reached 15 goals across three tournaments between 1998 and 2006. Cristiano Ronaldo of Portugal reached 11 once he scored in the round of 32 at the 2026 tournament, which the Opta Analyst describes as his first World Cup knockout goal.

## How the record has changed hands

FIFA keeps a progression of every player who has held the all-time record. It shows how rarely the title moves: some holders kept it for decades.

- Guillermo Stabile of Argentina, 8 goals in 1930, later shared with Leonidas of Brazil
- Ademir de Menezes of Brazil, 9 goals, until 1954
- Sándor Kocsis of Hungary, 11 goals, until 1958
- Just Fontaine of France, 13 goals, from 1958 to 1974
- Gerd Müller of West Germany, 14 goals, from 1974 to 2006
- Ronaldo of Brazil, 15 goals, from 2006 to 2014
- Miroslav Klose of Germany, 16 goals, from 2014 to 2026
- Lionel Messi of Argentina, 21 goals, from 16 June to 18 July 2026
- Kylian Mbappé of France, 22 goals, since 18 July 2026

Müller's total stood for 32 years, and Fontaine's lasted 16. Messi's reign was the shortest on the list: FIFA records that he became the joint record holder on 16 June 2026, the outright leader on 22 June, and lost the record on 18 July.

## What changed at the 2026 tournament

Two players rewrote the table within a single tournament. Messi scored a hat-trick against Algeria in Argentina's opening match to draw level with Klose on 16, and the Opta Analyst notes he became the first player to score at six different World Cups. FIFA lists him with eight goals in eight matches at the 2026 edition.

Mbappé scored ten in eight matches. He moved level with Müller, Ronaldo and Klose in the group stage, passed Klose in the round of 32, and overtook Messi with two goals in France's defeat to England in the bronze final on 18 July. That finished him on 22 goals from 22 matches, exactly one goal per match. FIFA also records that he became the first player since Müller in 1970 to reach double figures at a single tournament.

## Totals versus goals per match

Totals reward longevity and, increasingly, the length of the tournament. FIFA says explicitly that Mbappé benefited from playing eight matches at the expanded 48-team tournament, and the format guide explains [how the 48-team World Cup works](/guides/how-the-48-team-world-cup-format-works) and why a finalist can now play more games than before.

FIFA's progression page gives goals and matches for each record holder, which makes a per-match comparison possible:

- Fontaine: 13 goals in 6 matches, just over two per match
- Kocsis: 11 goals in 5 matches, also just over two per match
- Müller: 14 goals in 13 matches, a little over one per match
- Mbappé: 22 goals in 22 matches, one per match
- Ronaldo of Brazil: 15 goals in 19 matches, about 0.8 per match
- Klose: 16 goals in 24 matches, about 0.7 per match
- Messi: 21 goals in 34 matches, about 0.6 per match

By that measure the early scorers and Müller were far more prolific per game, while Messi and Klose built their totals over six and four tournaments respectively. Neither view is wrong. Totals measure a career at the World Cup, and the rate measures how often a scorer found the net when he played.

## The single-tournament record

Fontaine's 13 goals in six matches at the 1958 World Cup is still the record for one edition, according to FIFA. He scored in all six of his matches, including four against West Germany in the third-place play-off. The Opta Analyst adds that his 13 goals were more than double the next highest scorers that year and that he retired at 28 because of injury.

Only four players have reached double figures at one tournament: Fontaine, Kocsis with 11 in 1954, Müller with 10 in 1970 and, now, Mbappé with 10 in 2026. Kocsis, two goals short, is the closest anyone has come to Fontaine's record.

## The Golden Boot and what a winning total looks like

The Golden Boot, first awarded as the Golden Shoe in 1982 and renamed in 2010, goes to the tournament's top scorer. FIFA's list of winners shows how consistent the winning total used to be. From 1982 to 2018 the top scorer finished on five or six goals at every tournament except 2002, when Ronaldo scored eight. Müller won in 2010 on five goals after a tie was broken by assists, Klose won in 2006 with five, and Kane won in 2018 with six.

The recent winners look different: Mbappé's ten in 2026 equals Müller's winning total from 1970, and his eight in 2022 matched Ronaldo's eight in 2002. The longer 2026 tournament played a part in the higher figure, as noted above.
